# Part B (working text v2): other coloured labels and the colorimetric signal

Working text, 2026-11-10. Reframed around the question of this review: how does each non-gold coloured label change the colour signal of a strip, by how much, and at what cost? Each subsection follows four questions: what limits the signal, which label addresses it, what the papers report against their own baseline (usually a gold strip or a fluorescent label), and what the change costs. The target virus is named for every method. Numbers are in the tables; Table B4 gathers the within-paper comparisons. † = read in full; all other rows abstract only (NR = not reported in the abstract). Every DOI cited in the text appears in a table and in the evidence table. The section covers 33 included records (R07568 excluded as not a conventional LFA; pre-2015 records removed), all from 2015-2026; seven were read in full.

# 3.2 Other coloured labels: what a different colour carrier changes

## 3.2.0 What limits the signal of a gold strip, and how this section is organised

Three limits of the gold label are given in the papers of this section. The first is optics: polydopamine nanoparticles absorb over a broader and stronger band than gold, which the authors of a SARS-CoV-2 IgM/IgG strip used as the reason for the change (DOI: 10.3390/bios13030352). The second is manufacturing: colloidal gold strips are described as limited in analytical sensitivity and affected by batch-to-batch differences in particle size, while dyed latex beads are bright and uniform in size and can be coupled to antibodies in a controlled way, as argued for porcine epidemic diarrhea virus and porcine circovirus 2 strips (DOI: 10.1186/s44149-021-00029-1; DOI: 10.1007/s00253-022-12074-y). The third is information: particles of different colours let one strip report more than one analyte, as in a strip for African swine fever virus and classical swine fever virus antibodies (DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419).

The section follows the labels from the most developed to the least. Coloured latex microspheres come first (3.2.1), then colour coding for multiplexing (3.2.2), carbon (3.2.3), selenium, silver and other inorganic labels (3.2.4), organic and polymer chromophores (3.2.5) and cellulose nanobeads (3.2.6). Section 3.2.7 collects the studies in which a coloured label was less sensitive than a fluorescent one, and 3.2.8 states what the evidence supports.

**How the signal is read.** Reading the abstracts of the 33 records in this section, 18 state that the strip is read by eye, 0 state that a device is used (smartphone, reader, scanner or imager), 3 state either eye or device, 12 describe a strip without saying how it is read, and 0 has no abstract. 4 records report a second readout or a non-colour comparator (fluorescence, SERS, thermal, electrochemical); they are marked ‡ in the tables, and only the colorimetric channel is counted as a result of this review. "Not stated" means only that the abstract is silent. Records read in full give their reading mode from the full text (marked "full text" in the tables); the counts above refer to abstracts.

## 3.2.1 Coloured latex microspheres

Latex microspheres are polymer particles a few hundred nanometres in diameter that are dyed in a chosen colour and carry carboxyl groups for covalent coupling to antibodies (DOI: 10.1186/s44149-021-00029-1; DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419). Their size is a design constraint as well as an advantage. Large particles aggregate easily, so the amount of protein coupled to the surface has to be set at the highest level that does not cause sedimentation (DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419).

Most of the latex work in this review concerns animal viruses detected directly as antigen, and two studies read in full show what such strips can and cannot do. A red-latex strip for porcine circovirus 2 used monoclonal antibodies raised against the dominant genotypes and detected the three main genotypes, but was clearly less sensitive for the older PCV2a genotype than for PCV2b and PCV2d, which the authors attribute to the immunogens (DOI: 10.1007/s00253-022-12074-y). Against PCR on tissue and swab samples it was fully specific but missed some PCR-positive samples. The authors explain this partly by the fact that PCR detects viral DNA whether or not it is infectious virus (DOI: 10.1007/s00253-022-12074-y). A latex strip for porcine epidemic diarrhea virus in faeces solved a practical problem of the sample itself: solid faecal particles disturbed the strip, and a filter pad placed between the sample pad and the conjugate pad improved agreement with RT-PCR (DOI: 10.1186/s44149-021-00029-1). Both studies therefore illustrate that a coloured carrier alone does not set performance; antibody choice and sample handling matter as much.

Latex strips have also been made for SARS-CoV-2 antigen (25 ng/mL in positive specimens), for North American porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV; sensitivity 90.32% on clinical samples) and, with a sialylglycopolymer-latex probe, for the receptor specificity of influenza A group 1 viruses, where the strip correlated above 0.95 with an ELISA (DOI: 10.1002/jcla.24091; DOI: 10.3389/fmicb.2022.882112; DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2014.10.036). A latex double-antigen sandwich for SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibodies had a LOD of 7.11 ng/mL against 1.13 ng/mL for the fluorescent-bead version, with sensitivity 98.29% and specificity 97.70% against a virus neutralization test (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2022.124200). A latex strip for the SARS-CoV-2 N protein detected 10 ng/mL, against 15 pg/mL for a sandwich ELISA with the same antibody pair in the same study (DOI: 10.1111/1751-7915.70117). Composite carriers combine latex with a second material. Gold or platinum nanoparticle-latex nanocomposites were used for influenza virus, with enhanced sensitivity whose value the abstract does not give (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.8b11745); gold-coated polystyrene latex for influenza H3 reached 1/16 HAU by eye and 0.016 HAU quantitatively (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-020-04255-1); highly chromatic red silica nanoparticles doped with Direct Red 224 detected H7 avian influenza (H7N2 and H7N9) at 0.08 ng/mL (DOI: 10.1039/d1ay00204j); and dye-loaded polymersomes served as the carrier of a SARS-CoV-2 colorimetric strip (DOI: 10.1021/acssensors.1c00854). Their abstracts report mainly analytical data (Table B1a). The limit addressed in 3.2.1 is uniformity and brightness of the carrier; the cost is that sensitivity is set by the antibody pair and sample handling as much as by the colour, and the SARS-CoV-2 N-protein result above shows that a latex strip can be far less sensitive than a laboratory ELISA.

## 3.2.2 Colour as a code: multicolour strips for multiplexing

Because coloured carriers come in distinct hues, one strip can report more than one analyte and the result is read by colour. A strip with red, blue and black latex gives a red line for African swine fever virus (ASFV) antibodies, a blue line for classical swine fever virus (CSFV) antibodies, and a black control line (DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419). Its visual detection limit was equal to a commercial ELISA for ASFV and better than the commercial ELISA for CSFV, and agreement with the ELISAs on 180 sera was high for both (DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419). The authors point out a limit that no label can remove: CSFV E2 antibodies cannot separate vaccinated from infected animals, so the CSFV line mainly serves to evaluate vaccine response (DOI: 10.1155/tbed/5512419).

Other multicolour designs use a bicolour latex strip for SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibodies (13 serum samples, about 48 ng/mL) and a red/blue strip that reads a duplex RT-LAMP for human norovirus GII (5.8 copies/uL) and human adenovirus F41 (63 copies/uL) (DOI: 10.3390/bios12020103; DOI: 10.3389/fmicb.2026.1787049). A further design uses colour only to make the result easier to read: a red latex test line for ASFV antibodies and a blue latex control line made with chicken IgY, which read by eye within 15 min, agreed closely with a commercial ELISA on 159 sera and tolerated months of storage at 4 C, room temperature and 37 C (DOI: 10.1007/s00253-025-13694-w). In that study the authors found that larger particles (400 nm) gave a weak false-positive line, and settled on 300 nm (DOI: 10.1007/s00253-025-13694-w). Colour coding keeps the strip equipment-free, but it relies on the user telling hues apart, and none of these abstracts reports blinded or multi-reader reading (Table B1b).

## 3.2.3 Carbon-based labels

Carbon labels give a dark signal from a material very different from the metals. Carbon black was used as the label for a severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) strip with a detection limit of 100 pg/mL in spiked serum, and in a vertical-flow format for SARS-CoV-2 IgG, where the authors report a lower LOD than colloidal gold without giving the value (DOI: 10.1039/d3an00515a; DOI: 10.3390/bios13090857). The only head-to-head comparison with gold and clinical samples, for porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV) on 137 faecal samples, found the carbon strip less sensitive than the gold strip (1:64 against 1:128 at 10^8 TCID50/mL; agreement above 88% against 92.7%) (DOI: 10.1016/j.isci.2026.116948). The limit addressed is cost and the dark, high-contrast signal; the cost is that sensitivity was not improved in the one direct comparison (Table B1c).

## 3.2.4 Selenium, silver, ultramarine blue and magnetic labels

Several inorganic particles have been proposed as red or blue labels. Selenium nanoparticles were used for SARS-CoV-2 antibodies (IgM 20 ng/mL, IgG 5 ng/mL; sensitivity 93.33% and specificity 97.34% on 90 patients and 263 controls) and, with structured protein probes on a chip, for SARS-CoV-2 antigen in throat swab, saliva, serum and finger-prick blood (10 pg/mL; overall coincidence 95.83% with RT-PCR in 192 samples) (DOI: 10.1039/d0lc00828a; DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2024.116878); a second selenium antibody strip gives no values in its abstract (DOI: 10.1109/tnb.2021.3105662). Silver is a stronger absorber than gold but is unstable; coating the particles with a thin calixarene layer made them stable in plasma and for months of storage, and carboxyl groups on the coating allowed covalent coupling of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.2c00870). The particles give a yellow line, not a red one, and in a dipstick assay for anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG they gave a lower LOD and similar signals with ten times fewer particles than gold particles with adsorbed protein (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.2c00870). The gain was shown in buffer; in human plasma the LOD was several hundred-fold higher, and on 25 serum samples the dipstick was fully specific but detected only about three-quarters of the PCR-positive patients, which the authors say matches typical gold-based strips (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.2c00870). Ultramarine blue, an inexpensive inorganic pigment, was used as a blue label for the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), with a LOD of 0.37 ng/mL, and read by eye or by image analysis; it was tested in spiked fetal calf serum and not in patient samples. The abstract states a lower LOD than a gold strip, whereas the main-text comparisons found are with literature values for iron-oxide and red-silica labels, and a quantum-dot method is described as more sensitive (DOI: 10.1016/j.aca.2019.11.037). Magnetic oxide labels combine colour with a magnetic core: a natural-colour Fe3O4 strip for SARS-CoV-2 reached 0.5 ng/mL (DOI: 10.1016/j.ijbiomac.2026.150960). All of these are known from abstracts only. A head-to-head gold comparator is reported for the silver and ultramarine-blue strips (Table B1d).

## 3.2.5 Organic and polymer chromophores

Polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles show how a new label needs surface engineering as much as gold does. PDA sticks strongly to nitrocellulose, and probes coupled directly to it would be trapped at the test line and give false negatives; the authors therefore coated the particles with polyelectrolyte layers, silica and PEG-silane before coupling antigen and antibody (DOI: 10.3390/bios13030352). The resulting three-line strip separated IgG and IgM and was quantified with a camera and image software. In the same study a commercial gold strip barely detected low antibody concentrations (DOI: 10.3390/bios13030352). The study used serum spiked with standard antibodies, not patient samples. Related labels include black polylevodopa nanoparticles for SARS-CoV-2 (5 pg/mL) and influenza A H1N1 (0.1 ng/mL), (DOI: 10.1016/j.bbrc.2024.149821), and polydiacetylene vesicles that show a red line and red fluorescence at the same time, used for HBsAg: the red line was confirmed by naked eye up to 1 ng/mL, whereas the fluorescent image reached 0.1 ng/mL, so only the 1 ng/mL figure is a colorimetric result (DOI: 10.1002/asia.201700769). The abstracts give little accuracy data (Table B1e).

## 3.2.6 Cellulose nanobeads

Cellulose nanobeads are dyed cellulose particles. They were used for SARS-CoV-2 antigen with an scFv-Fc binder (2 ng antigen; 2.5 x 10^4 pfu of cultured virus) (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2020.112868), for a comparison with a 40 nm gold strip for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid against the WHO target product profile, where the cellulose cut-off was 100 times below the 10^6 copies/mL profile threshold and the gold strip met it (DOI: 10.1039/d5ra08197a), and for Nipah virus nucleoprotein in inactivated cultures, with 1 ng/mL nucleoprotein, 4.1 x 10^7 copies/mL by reader and 8.3 x 10^7 by naked eye (DOI: 10.1080/22221751.2026.2716499). They are the most recent group here and the evidence is limited to analytical data (Table B1f).

## 3.2.7 Where coloured labels fall short

Several studies compare a coloured label with a fluorescent label and show the cost of equipment-free reading. Fluorescent microspheres were more sensitive than coloured microspheres in strips for rotavirus and adenovirus and for influenza A and B (DOI: 10.1186/s12985-018-0951-5; DOI: 10.1186/s12985-019-1190-0). In a SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing-antibody strip the fluorescent version reached a lower LOD than the latex colorimetric version (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2022.124200). For antibodies against the human adenovirus 3 hexon protein, quantum dots reached the higher positive agreement of the two labels tested (100% against 96.9% for gold) (DOI: 10.3390/diagnostics16162670). These results are consistent with a trade-off, in which coloured labels give up some sensitivity in exchange for reading without a fluorescence reader (Table B1g).

## 3.2.8 What the evidence supports

For the question of this review, the answer is that non-gold coloured labels mainly change what the strip can do, not how strong the colour is. Coloured latex is the best-documented alternative to gold, mainly in veterinary virology (porcine epidemic diarrhea virus, porcine circovirus 2, PRRSV, African and classical swine fever virus), and multicolour latex lets several analytes share a test region and be told apart by colour. Where a comparison with gold exists in the same paper, the reported gain in the colour signal is small or absent: silver was one order of magnitude lower in LOD in buffer for SARS-CoV-2 IgG but several hundred-fold worse in plasma; polydopamine quantified low SARS-CoV-2 antibody concentrations better than a commercial gold strip; cellulose nanobeads reached a lower cut-off than gold for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid; and carbon was less sensitive than gold for porcine deltacoronavirus (Table B4). Against fluorescent labels the coloured labels were consistently less sensitive. The other labels rest largely on single studies and abstracts.

Three limitations recur. First, reference methods differ between studies (PCR, RT-PCR, commercial ELISA, virus neutralization), so agreement values cannot be compared across them. Second, several studies used spiked or standard samples only. Third, genotype or strain coverage depends on the antibody pair and not on the label.
