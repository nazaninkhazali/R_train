# 3.3 Catalytic and enzymatic amplification: adding colour after capture

## 3.3.1 From a better label to a second source of colour

Sections 3.1 and 3.2 improved the colour of a strip by changing the label: its size, its shape, its material. Whatever the label, however, the colour of a conventional strip remains limited by two quantities, what each captured particle absorbs and how many particles the antibodies manage to capture (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). Amplification attacks the problem from the other side. It leaves the label as it is and adds a second source of colour at the test line after the sandwich has formed: something captured there either deposits more material or turns over a substrate into a coloured product, so that each captured label produces far more colour than it carries. This is the principle of the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and in the laboratory it is routine; on a strip, however, conventional enzyme amplification demands technical skill and equipment that suit field use poorly (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). The studies of this section ask how far catalytic amplification can be brought into a strip while keeping it simple enough for the point of need.

They can be read as a progression in how much of the amplifier is built into the strip and how stable it is. The simplest amplifier uses the captured gold itself as a seed for further metal deposition, and it continues the enlargement strategies of Section 3.1.3 (Section 3.3.2). The next uses a natural enzyme as the label, with a substrate that the enzyme converts into colour (Section 3.3.3). Natural enzymes, however, lose activity in storage, and the third step replaces them with inorganic particles that mimic enzyme activity, nanozymes, first as noble-metal particles (Section 3.3.4), then as engineered structures with supports, magnetic cores and several metals (Section 3.3.5), and finally as non-noble catalysts and catalysts paired with a new binder (Section 3.3.6). All of these amplifiers cost a step, and the fourth part of the section follows the attempts to build the step into the strip so that the user adds nothing but the sample (Section 3.3.7). A last group adds a second, instrumental readout to the catalytic colour, and only the colour is counted here (Section 3.3.8); Section 3.3.9 states what the evidence supports.

The rules of the earlier sections apply: a change is an improvement only when the same study runs an unamplified or conventional strip beside it, the target virus is named for every method, and only the colour channel is evaluated. The section covers 49 included records from 2015 to 2026 after the exclusion of seven evaluations of marketed amplification kits (rule E10); 22 were read in full, and the figures of the others are given as their abstracts state them (Tables C1a to C1f; Table C4 collects the within-study comparisons).

## 3.3.2 Depositing more metal on captured gold

The simplest amplifier is the particle itself. Gold that has been captured at the test line can be enlarged by depositing silver, gold or copper on it, which darkens the line without any new label, and the idea was introduced for gold in Section 3.1.3. The silver version lowered the detection limit of an equine influenza virus strip 5-fold against conventional rapid tests (DOI: 10.1292/jvms.17-0105). For foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) it was built into a device in which silver grows on the captured gold from 0.05 to 10 um, about 200 times in diameter, and black lines appear within 5 minutes after the reagents are released. For the O/TUR/2009 strain this gave a detection limit of 10^4 TCID50/0.1 mL against 10^5 for the commercial gold kit, and 10- to 100-fold for most serotypes, although the gain was not seen for serotypes SAT2 and SAT3. The same study shows what real samples can do to an amplifier: in 132 samples from an outbreak in Mongolia, read against a colour chart, sensitivity against rRT-PCR was 85.4% on vesicular epithelium but 46.7% on lesion swabs and 36.8% on saliva, and saliva inhibited the untreated test in 51.3% of samples (DOI: 10.1016/j.jviromet.2019.113736).

Gold and copper reach further. Gold enhancement lowered the limit of an infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV) strip from 0.25 to 0.050 ug/mL, a fivefold gain (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2026.130562). Copper deposition was the most powerful of the three. For HIV-1 p24, a copper shell grown in situ on the captured gold with the help of polyethylenimine, which fixes the copper ions at the particle and avoids their nonspecific binding to protein and membrane, lowered the visual limit from 2 ng/mL to 50 fg/mL, over four orders of magnitude, and the same step improved a model streptavidin-gold assay from 3 pM to 0.3 fM. It also showed its value on a marketed strip: a commercial gold antibody strip for SARS-CoV-2 missed 7 of 69 PCR-confirmed positive blood samples (specificity 100% in 63 negatives), and all 7 became positive after amplification while the negatives stayed negative (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2020.112753; the bacterial result in the same paper is not counted). For the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, copper deposition for 3 minutes turned the red band black and lowered the visual limit from 0.01 ug/mL to 10 pg/mL, three orders of magnitude, but only with certified reference antigen: the authors state that no cultured virus or real samples were tested (DOI: 10.3390/bios12010013). A three-dimensional device combines polyA gold nanoparticles with gold and silver deposition for SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid, but its abstract gives no values (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03575). One related design redesigned the gold-nanosphere strip for the SARS-CoV-2 spike receptor-binding domain and added a thermal-contrast reading step of under one minute: the visual limits were 3.6 aM in buffer and 28.6 aM in nasopharyngeal wash, and the thermal step distinguished 26 to 32% of the visual false negatives of commercial strips, a gain that is thermal, not colorimetric (DOI: 10.1021/acsanm.1c03217).

Metal deposition is therefore a large and cheap gain, but it carries the cost that Section 3.1.3 described: an added reagent step and a risk of background. The remaining question is whether the step can be made part of the strip, and the answer, discussed in Section 3.3.7, came first for gold, with a self-enhancing triplex strip.

## 3.3.3 Natural enzymes as labels

A natural enzyme amplifies in a different way. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) turn over their substrates repeatedly, so that one captured label produces many coloured product molecules, and on a strip they have been used alone, combined with gold, or on magnetic carriers. The clearest demonstration of what the enzyme adds to gold comes from potato virus X. Adding ALP on a second gold conjugate lowered the LOD to 0.3 ng/mL, 27-fold below the 8 ng/mL of the conventional strip with the same antibodies and the same 20 nm gold, in buffer and in potato leaf extract, and the line changed from red to dark violet, which gives better contrast on white nitrocellulose. By contrast, adding the second gold conjugate without the enzyme gained only 1.5-fold, so the enzyme, not the extra gold, carried the gain (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-017-2595-3). The choice of enzyme matters for the sample: the authors note that the endogenous peroxidase activity of plant extracts limits the use of HRP enhancement, and they pair gold with ALP, the second most common enzyme of commercial ELISA kits, for that reason (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-017-2595-3).

In human virology HRP with the chromogen TMB has been tested at scale. An HRP-labelled strip for influenza A and B, in which TMB is added to a substrate pad after the sample and the whole procedure takes 20 minutes, detected 8 influenza A isolates at 0.001 to 0.00025 HAU and 5 influenza B isolates at 0.016 to 0.004 HAU, more than one order of magnitude below BD gold strips (0.01 to 0.04 HAU and 0.32 to 0.04 HAU). Against rRT-PCR the clinical figures depended strongly on how the sample was taken: sensitivity for influenza A was 90.2% (55/61) on nasopharyngeal swabs but 66.2% (45/68) on oropharyngeal swabs, for influenza B 82.6% (71/86) and 58.4% (45/77), with specificity of 99.7% or above throughout; the assignment of these figures to the two viruses is reconstructed from the counts, because the text of the paper is interleaved (DOI: 10.1002/jmv.25322). Several more strips follow the same pattern. HRP added to a colloidal gold strip for bovine parvovirus gave a tenfold gain over the traditional strip (10^2 TCID50) on faecal samples (DOI: 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1174737); a magnetic enzyme label detected dengue virus NS1 of serotypes 1 to 4 at 0.1 to 1.0 ng/mL with 100% sensitivity and specificity on clinical samples (DOI: 10.7717/peerj.7779); a capture-layer design with an enzyme label detected dengue NS1 with 5-fold higher sensitivity than a gold-standard ELISA (DOI: 10.1021/acsomega.0c00367); a hepatitis B e-antigen strip with an amplification system of unstated mechanism was 27-fold more sensitive than its conventional strip, in 420 blood samples (DOI: 10.1002/jmv.25452); and HRP tracers detected hepatitis B virus DNA in a vertical-flow format with 100% consistency with real-time PCR (DOI: 10.1007/s00216-022-03988-7).

The most inventive use of HRP deposits the product not as a precipitate but as a second colour. In tyramide signal amplification the enzyme converts biotin-tyramide into radicals that bind to nearby proteins, and a second round with streptavidin-HRP microspheres multiplies the label. For the SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein the single-colour version gave a naked-eye cut-off of 31.3 pg/mL, which the authors compare favourably with values quoted from the literature for two commercial antigen tests (121 and 204 pg/mL); the dual-colour version reached an LOD of 3.98 pg/mL against 55.46 pg/mL for the traditional strip, about 14-fold, with a naked-eye cut-off of 7.8 pg/mL against 125 pg/mL, 16-fold. Inactivated virus was detected at 0.018 TCID50/mL, seven variants including Omicron BA.1 and BA.2 at 5 TCID50/mL, and no signal arose with influenza A and B, parainfluenza 2 or respiratory syncytial virus; no patient samples were tested (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2024.116262).

The weakness of natural enzymes is stability. In one direct test, freeze-dried antibody-HRP conjugates lost most of their signal within two weeks of dry storage at room temperature, whereas a platinum nanocatalyst kept its signal for weeks, even at 44 C (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). That observation is the main reason the field moved to the particles of the next section. The limit addressed at this level is the number of coloured product molecules per captured label; the cost is a substrate step and a label that loses activity in dry storage.

## 3.3.4 Noble-metal nanozymes: from a model to a strip

Nanozymes are particles with enzyme-like activity. Supplied with hydrogen peroxide and a chromogenic substrate, a peroxidase-like nanozyme oxidises the substrate and deposits colour at the test line, exactly as HRP does, but with the stability of an inorganic material. Platinum-based particles dominate the evidence, and the most thoroughly characterised example set the pattern for the rest.

A porous platinum core-shell nanocatalyst for the HIV-1 p24 antigen reached 0.8 pg/mL (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). Its activity depends on particle size, on the polymer capping used in synthesis and on the porous platinum shell, which stays accessible to hydrogen peroxide even when serum proteins coat the surface; the best size range was 120 to 200 nm, and larger particles performed worse. The same strip works in two regimes, because at high antigen the intrinsic dark colour of the particle is visible and at low antigen the catalytic step takes over, which widens the dynamic range over several orders of magnitude. In serial plasma from an HIV seroconversion panel it detected antigen during the acute phase, and lyophilised reagents survived weeks of accelerated ageing; but the assay needs a wash step and a 5-minute development, so it is not a one-step strip (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229).

Gold-platinum particles carry the principle to SARS-CoV-2. In a nanoenzyme-linked sensor for the nucleocapsid protein, Au@Pt particles gave an LOD of 0.026 ng/mL and a positivity of 76.2% against 47.6% for ELISA in clinical samples; the developed substrate is read by transmitted light in a small 3D-printed holder with a laser, a photometer and a smartphone, so the result is colorimetric but not equipment-free (DOI: 10.1016/j.snb.2021.130718). The LOD was similar to a laboratory ELISA and lower than that of a conventional strip with the same antibodies, and the Au@Pt particles kept their activity over days while HRP lost it (DOI: 10.1016/j.snb.2021.130718). The first simple strips followed. Au@Pt particles with a 2-minute TMB step lowered the naked-eye limit for the nucleocapsid protein from 50 ng/mL without catalysis to 40 pg/mL, about 1250-fold (the paper says more than 1000-fold), and the result in artificial saliva did not differ from that in water; as the authors note, no clinical samples were tested (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-024-06697-3). Au@Pt@Pd, a trimetallic particle, shows the limits of attributing a gain to the amplifier: the LOD was 0.24 ng/mL before catalysis and 0.06 ng/mL after, so catalysis added only 4-fold, and the 16-fold and 8-fold advantage over a gold strip (0.98 ng/mL) and a gold-flower strip (0.49 ng/mL) therefore comes mostly from the probe itself, with an ELISA at 0.12 ng/mL; in 44 positive and 50 negative nasopharyngeal swabs the sensitivity was 84.09%, the specificity 100% and the accuracy 92.55% (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-024-06819-x). A related Au@Pd@Pt strip reached 0.037 ng/mL for the same antigen, 17-fold below the unenhanced strip (DOI: 10.1039/d2ay01530g).

The same particles have been tried on other targets. Peroxide-tolerant Au@Pt served for potato virus X (4 pg/mL in tuber, 8 pg/mL in leaf) (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2020.121961), oxidase-like Au@Pt nanorods for hepatitis B virus DNA needed no hydrogen peroxide (8.5 pM) (DOI: 10.1039/d1an02084f), an AuPt nanoalloy was used for human papillomavirus 16 (HPV16) DNA (DOI: 10.1039/d3ra00757j), Au@Pt for respiratory syncytial virus F protein reached 0.78 ng/mL, about 4-fold better than colloidal gold (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2026.130820), Pt-Pd particles for foot-and-mouth disease virus serotype O detected 96.7% of positive sera (DOI: 10.1016/j.jchromb.2024.124043), PdRu nanocages gave 0.93 and 0.19 nM for HPV16 and HPV18 (DOI: 10.1016/j.jcis.2024.06.002), and polydopamine-platinum particles for influenza A gave a 2-fold colorimetric and a 200-fold catalytic gain against a gold strip (DOI: 10.1186/s12951-025-03903-z). A platinum-polymer nanoparticle strip for SARS-CoV-2 evaluated on 200 clinical specimens had a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 100% (DOI: 10.3390/biomedicines13122993), a reminder that amplification does not remove the limits that antibody pairs and sample type set (Table C1c). The limit addressed at this level is turnover per label; the cost is peroxide and substrate, a development step and, in the best-characterised case, a wash step. The question that the next studies pose is how much more catalytic activity can be placed on each captured label.

## 3.3.5 Engineering the nanozyme: supports, magnets and several metals

A nanozyme can only turn over substrate at its surface, so the obvious way to make it stronger is to put more catalytic surface on each probe. The studies of this group do it by giving the particle a support with a large surface, a magnetic core that also concentrates the virus, and a combination of metals, and they show that the three work together.

The support comes first. A platinum nanozyme grown on a mesoporous silica shell around an Fe3O4 core, for influenza A, has a larger specific surface than a version with a non-porous shell, and with DAB as substrate the visual limit was 1 ng/mL before catalysis (10-fold below the 10 ng/mL of a commercial gold strip) and 0.01 ng/mL after catalysis, a further 100-fold; the quantitative LOD was 0.0089 ng/mL, 112-fold lower than for the non-porous version, and 33 copies/mL for inactivated H1N1, with the naked-eye limit moving from 10^4 to 50 copies/mL (200-fold). Recovery in spiked throat swabs was 95.20 to 106.00% and no patient samples were tested; the abstract states that the sensitivity is about 100 times that of commercial gold strips, whereas the visual figures of the text would give 1000 times, and both are reported as stated (DOI: 10.1016/j.aca.2024.343474). The same logic with MoS2 as support: Fe3O4@MoS2@Pt nanotags for SARS-CoV-2 and H1N1 had a visual limit of 1 ng/mL before catalysis, equal to that of a commercial gold strip (1 ng/mL) and 10-fold below a gold strip made with the same antibodies (10 ng/mL), and 0.1 ng/mL after catalysis; the colour LOD was 80 pg/mL (the abstract prints ng/mL) and, for H1N1, 0.1 ug/mL visual before and 0.05 ug/mL after catalysis, against 1 ug/mL for a commercial gold strip (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.3c15851). A three-dimensional film-like GO-Pt-AuPt nanozyme detected two respiratory viruses at 1 pg/mL (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.4c09513).

The magnetic core adds a second gain that does not come from catalysis at all. In a multi-metallic nanozyme on MoS2 with Fe3O4/Ag satellites, magnetic separation concentrates influenza A virus from the throat swab before the strip, and catalysis then raised the visual limit from 0.05 ng/mL to 5 pg/mL, tenfold, to an LOD of 0.8 pg/mL; the version without the Fe3O4/Ag (MoS2@AP) had an LOD of 10 pg/mL and the nanofibre label alone (AP) 100 pg/mL, ten and one hundred times higher. Three commercial gold strips read 100 pg/mL visually, which is 125 times the calculated LOD but only 20 times the visual limit after catalysis, a distinction to keep in mind, and 20 clinical throat swabs were all classified correctly, with inactivated virus detected at 140 copies/mL within 26 minutes (DOI: 10.1002/exp.20240425). The most elaborate design, a rambutan-like Fe3O4 core with two Au@Ir layers and wheat germ agglutinin, which binds viral and bacterial glycoproteins broadly, separates these gains: for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, magnetic enrichment alone lowered the visual limit from 0.1 to 0.01 ng/mL, the second Au@Ir layer made the catalysis 5-fold stronger than a single layer, and catalysis took the visual limit from 0.5 to 0.01 ng/mL (50-fold) and the LOD from 0.1099 to 0.0018 ng/mL. Against a gold strip (about 1 ng/mL) the colour mode is 2- to 10-fold better and the catalytic LOD at least 555-fold better, and against an ELISA (0.21 ng/mL) 116-fold; sensitivity and specificity were both 100% in 23 positive and 9 negative throat swabs (the probe also detects two bacteria in the same strip, which are not counted) (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03762). A core-satellite magnetic nanozyme whose Pt and Ir protrusions mimic the spikes of a coronavirus for SARS-CoV-2 and mpox virus antigens confirmed the arithmetic: catalysis lowered the visual limit 100-fold (5 to 0.05 ng/mL, LOD 1.80 and 2.09 ng/mL to 19.4 and 22.5 pg/mL), magnetic enrichment added tenfold, and against gold strips of 5 and 10 ng/mL the overall gain was 1000- to 4000-fold; in 117 throat swabs the direct mode reached a sensitivity of 89.04% (kappa 0.859) and the enrichment mode 100% (73/73) against qPCR (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.6c03394).

Taken together, these studies give the section its most useful quantitative result. For a nanozyme of this kind the catalytic step adds about 10- to 100-fold to the visual limit of the same strip, magnetic enrichment adds about 10-fold, a better support or a second metal layer adds 5-fold or more, and what is advertised against a commercial gold strip is the product of these gains and the brightness of the probe itself, measured sometimes on the visual limit and sometimes on a calculated LOD.

## 3.3.6 Non-noble catalysts, and catalysts paired with a new binder

Platinum is expensive, and iron oxide was the first alternative. A Fe3O4 nanozyme strip for Ebola virus glycoprotein, developed with DAB and peroxide after the run, reached 1 ng/mL by eye against 100 ng/mL for a standard colloidal gold strip and 0.1 ng/mL for an ELISA (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2015.05.025). Because no Ebola patient samples were available, the authors tested pseudovirus in serum and, as a surrogate, serum from 20 PCR-positive and 31 negative patients with a different bunyavirus infection; the strip detected 12 of 20 positives against 8 of 20 for the ELISA, with no false positives (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2015.05.025). The potato virus study that followed used Fe3O4 very differently. The particles were applied as a plain colorimetric label read by eye within 10 minutes, with no substrate step, to build monovalent and bivalent strips for potato viruses X and Y; the authors note that substrate-based amplification could raise the sensitivity further but did not test it, all 12 tissue-culture plantlets matched RT-PCR, and storage stability and batch consistency were not evaluated (DOI: 10.3390/plants15040656). The two studies show that the term "nanozyme strip" covers both amplified and non-amplified formats.

Other catalysts change the chemistry and, in one case, the binder. A PDA@MnO2 oxidase-like mimic was paired with a peptide, WFLNDSELISML, obtained by phage biopanning and binding the spike protein, in place of an antibody. Against a gold strip with the same peptide the visual limit was 500 pg/mL, against 100 pg/mL for the PDA@MnO2 probe without catalysis and 50 pg/mL with it, 10-fold below gold; the abstract's 18.7-fold lower LOD is not reproduced by the printed LODs (15.0, 12.8 and 8.01 pg/mL), and the visual 10-fold is the reliable figure. Pseudovirus in saliva was visible at 300 TU/mL and at 150 TU/mL after catalysis, and no patient samples were tested (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.3c02821). The binder and the catalyst, in other words, are independent levers, as Sections 3.1.6 and 3.2 anticipated. Bimetallic Fe-Co particles were used for porcine rotavirus on faecal samples (100 TCID50/mL, 2-fold better than commercial gold strips; sensitivity 95.45%, specificity 100%) (DOI: 10.1016/j.ab.2026.116259), and a nanozyme strip for avian leukosis virus reached 12.5 TCID50/mL without cross-reaction with six avian viruses (DOI: 10.1016/j.tvjl.2025.106508) (Table C1d). The limit addressed at this level is the cost, stability and reactivity of the catalyst; the cost remains a substrate step.

## 3.3.7 Building the amplification into the strip

Every amplifier discussed so far costs a step, and the step is why many simple gold strips remain in use. The last group of studies tries to remove it. They do so in two ways: by delivering the reagents through the strip in the right sequence, or by delaying their release until the labels have been captured.

The gold version came first. A self-enhancing triplex strip for influenza A, influenza B and SARS-CoV-2 laminates two enhancement pads carrying Au3+ and hydroxylamine beneath the detection layer, separated by a double-sided adhesive barrier, so that gold grows in situ after the immunoreaction without a user step, and the growth is confined to the captured particles, which limits background. The visual limits were 2 ng/mL for influenza A, 2 ng/mL for influenza B and 0.2 ng/mL for SARS-CoV-2, against 10, 20 and 10 ng/mL for the conventional strip, gains of 5-, 10- and 50-fold (the abstract's 5- to 25-fold refers to the calculated LODs of 1.0, 1.0 and 0.2 ng/mL). In 172 samples, 132 standard-added and 40 paired, sensitivity was 97.0%, 97.2% and 98.3% with 100% specificity, though these are antigen-spiked samples; 24 residual RT-qPCR-positive swabs (8 per virus) were all detected for influenza A and SARS-CoV-2 and mostly for influenza B, and more than 85% of the signal remained after 21 days at 45 C, which the authors estimate as at least three months of storage (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2025.129228).

For nanozymes, the chromogenic substrate itself has to wait. A strip with delayed substrate release keeps the substrate in a glass-fibre membrane at the sample pad, so that it dissolves and reaches the test line only after the nanozyme probes have been captured, which lowers the background and removes a user step; it detected a synthetic influenza A (H1N1) nucleic acid spiked into serum with a 25-fold lower LOD than the same strip before catalysis, and has not been tested on patient samples (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2025.127557). A hydrogel-integrated strip traps DAB in a sodium alginate hydrogel on a glass-fibre membrane and releases it after about 4 minutes. With the SARS-CoV-2 E gene nucleic acid as a model target, the visual LOD was 0.2 nM, 25-fold lower than the common strip without amplification (5 nM), and the automatic release matched the manual addition of DAB (calculated LOD 0.146 against 0.150 nM); it was tested in artificial saliva spiked at 10, 50 and 100 nM, without cross-signal from RSV, influenza B, adenovirus and H1N1 sequences, and without patient samples (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-025-07731-8). A platinum strip for the influenza A nucleoprotein reaches the same goal with a flow-delay pad and an acidic secondary flow that deliver TMB and peroxide after the sample, so that the user adds nothing but the sample and a smartphone application reads the intensity ratio; the LOD was 11.6 pg/mL, 1000-fold lower than the traditional strip, and against RT-PCR on 42 clinical samples tested in triplicate the sensitivity was 96.8% and the specificity 98.4%, though these figures count the 126 replicate measurements, not 42 independent patients (DOI: 10.1021/acsinfecdis.5c00731). A nanozyme strip for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein with a push-button enhancement in a 3D-printed casing reached 0.1 ng/mL in undiluted serum, with the step reduced to one press (DOI: 10.1039/d1an01350e). An aptamer-based design for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein changes how the chemical signal is transformed and raised the signal quality more than 20-fold with an LOD of 168 pg/mL in 25 clinical samples (DOI: 10.1039/d4nr04130e). The limit addressed at this level is the operator, and the cost is a more complex strip assembly and, so far, very little patient data (Table C1e).

## 3.3.8 Catalytic colour with a second readout, and mixed targets

Several nanozyme strips add a second readout, such as photothermal, fluorescent or Raman signals, or combine viral and bacterial targets, and in each only the colour channel and the viral target are counted here. A Fe3O4@MoS2@Pt nanotag read in a catalytic colorimetric mode and in a photothermal mode, discussed in Section 3.3.5, is the clearest example, and its photothermal limits are not counted (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.3c15851). Others combine nanozymes with fluorescence or SERS: a SQF@ZIF-8/Pt hybrid for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (colorimetric 0.0302 ng/mL) (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c02780), a SERS-nanozyme strip for norovirus (0.001 ng/mL visual) and adenovirus (10^2 PFU/mL visual) in 92 stool samples (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03047), and a trimodal strip for norovirus (visual 0.05 ng/mL) and E. coli O157 in 116 stool samples, consistent with qPCR (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.6c07352). A FeAu@AuIr nanozyme strip for SARS-CoV-2 and two bacteria, tested on 170 positive respiratory samples, completes the group, with no values in its abstract (DOI: 10.1002/advs.202502574). Only the colour channel and the viral target are counted for this review (Table C1f).

## 3.3.9 What the evidence supports

Catalytic amplification gives the largest changes in the colour signal of this review. Within their own papers, the reported gains on the visual limit range from about twofold to four orders of magnitude, and they fall into three levels. Metal deposition and natural enzymes give mostly 5- to 100-fold gains with the same label (silver 5- to 100-fold, gold 5- to 50-fold, alkaline phosphatase 27-fold, tyramide 14- to 16-fold), and copper reaches over four orders on a model antigen. Nanozyme catalysis adds about 10- to 100-fold on the visual limit of the same strip, and, in engineered probes, magnetic enrichment adds another 10-fold and a better support or a second metal layer 5-fold or more. What is advertised against a commercial gold strip, between 16- and 4000-fold, is the product of these gains and the brightness of the probe, and it is measured sometimes on the visual limit and sometimes on a calculated LOD (Table C4). Nanozymes are consistently described as more stable than natural enzymes, which is the main reason for their use (Table C2).

Three cautions run through the evidence. First, amplification is paid for with steps: substrate, hydrogen peroxide, washing or a reading device; the clearest example is the smartphone nanoenzyme sensor for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, whose result depends on an optical reader, and the built-in designs of Section 3.3.7 are the answer to it. Second, the comparator differs (an unamplified strip, a commercial gold strip, the same strip before catalysis, an ELISA), so fold-improvements cannot be ranked. Third, the clinical evidence is thin and does not always hold up: where patient samples were tested, sensitivity ranged from 12 of 20 samples for the Ebola-surrogate strip and 36.8% for foot-and-mouth disease on saliva through 74% for a platinum-polymer SARS-CoV-2 strip to 100% with panels of about 30 samples, several designs were tested on antigen or spiked samples only, and replicate measurements were sometimes counted as separate samples. The potato virus Fe3O4 strip shows, finally, that a nanozyme can also serve as a plain label without any amplification step, which makes the term "nanozyme strip" ambiguous. The reporting quality of the 22 studies read in full is summarised in Table C3.
