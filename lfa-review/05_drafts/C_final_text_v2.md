# Part C (working text v2): catalytic and enzymatic amplification and the colorimetric signal

Working text, 2026-11-10. Reframed around the question of this review: how does catalytic amplification change the colour signal of a strip, by how much, and at what cost? Each subsection follows four questions: what limits the signal, which amplifier addresses it, what the papers report against their own baseline, and what it costs. The target virus is named for every method. Numbers are in the tables; Table C4 gathers the within-paper changes in the colour signal. † = read in full; all other rows abstract only (NR = not reported in the abstract). # 3.3 Catalytic and enzymatic amplification: adding colour after capture

## 3.3.0 What limits the colour, and how this section is organised

The colour of a conventional strip is limited by what the label itself absorbs, and by how many labels the antibodies manage to capture (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). Amplification adds a second source of colour: after the sandwich has formed, something at the test line produces or deposits more of it. Enzyme-catalysed amplification is standard in laboratory immunoassays, but conventional implementations need technical skill and equipment that suit field use poorly (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). The studies in this section try to bring catalytic amplification into the strip while keeping it simple enough for the point of need.

The approaches can be read as a progression in how much of the amplifier is built into the strip. The first group deposits metal on particles that are already captured (3.3.1). The second uses natural enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase and alkaline phosphatase as labels (3.3.2). The third replaces enzymes with inorganic nanozymes, particles that mimic enzyme activity and are described by their authors as more stable and easier to scale up (3.3.3 and 3.3.4; DOI: 10.1016/j.snb.2021.130718; DOI: 10.3390/plants15040656). The fourth tries to make the amplification step automatic (3.3.5). The last group combines catalytic colour with a second readout, and only the colorimetric channel counts here (3.3.6). Section 3.3.7 states what the evidence supports. Seven evaluations of marketed amplification kits were excluded under the rule agreed for commercial-kit evaluations (E10); they are listed in the supplement.

## 3.3.1 Depositing more metal on captured particles

The simplest amplifier uses the particles themselves as seeds for further metal deposition. Silver enhancement lowered the detection limit of an equine influenza virus strip 5-fold against conventional rapid tests, and of a foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) strip covering seven serotypes 10- to 100-fold against a commercial strip (DOI: 10.1292/jvms.17-0105; DOI: 10.1016/j.jviromet.2019.113736). Gold enhancement lowered the LOD of an infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV) strip from 0.25 to 0.050 ug/mL, a 5-fold gain (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2026.130562). Copper deposition on gold seeds extended the approach to the HIV-1 p24 antigen (50 fg/mL, in a strip that also detected Escherichia coli O157:H7) and to the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (a three-order range down to 10 pg/mL) (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2020.112753; DOI: 10.3390/bios12010013). A three-dimensional device combines polyA gold nanoparticles with gold and silver deposition in one step for SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid, but its abstract gives no values (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03575). These designs follow the logic described in section 3.1.2: the gain is large, but each needs an extra reagent step and carries a risk of background. One related approach amplifies a SARS-CoV-2 antigen strip by thermal contrast, with limits stated between femtomolar and attomolar; this changes the readout from colour to heat and so lies at the edge of this review (DOI: 10.1021/acsanm.1c03217). The limit addressed in 3.3.1 is the size of the captured particle; the cost is an added reagent step and background risk.

## 3.3.2 Natural enzymes as labels

Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) produce a coloured precipitate from a substrate. On a strip they have been used as the sole label, together with gold, or on magnetic carriers. ALP combined with gold nanoparticles lowered the LOD of a potato virus X strip to 0.3 ng/mL, 27-fold below a conventional strip (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-017-2595-3). An HRP-labelled strip for influenza A and B was more than 10-fold more sensitive than gold-colloidal strips on 13 isolates, with sensitivity 90.2% (55/61) and 82.6% (71/86) in nasopharyngeal samples (DOI: 10.1002/jmv.25322), and HRP added to a colloidal-gold strip for bovine parvovirus gave a 10-fold gain over the traditional strip (10^2 TCID50) on faecal samples (DOI: 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1174737). A magnetic enzyme label detected dengue virus NS1 of serotypes 1 to 4 at 0.1 to 1.0 ng/mL with 100% sensitivity and specificity on clinical samples, and a capture-layer design with an enzyme label detected dengue NS1 with 5-fold higher sensitivity than a gold-standard ELISA (DOI: 10.7717/peerj.7779; DOI: 10.1021/acsomega.0c00367). A hepatitis B e-antigen strip used a signal amplification system, with a mechanism not stated in the abstract, and was 27-fold more sensitive than the conventional strip (DOI: 10.1002/jmv.25452). Enzyme-induced tyramide deposition produced a dual-colour amplification for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein and influenza A/B (3.9 pg/mL; naked-eye cut-off 7.8 pg/mL) (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2024.116262), and HRP tracers detected hepatitis B virus DNA in a vertical-flow format with 100% consistency with real-time PCR (DOI: 10.1007/s00216-022-03988-7).

The main weakness of natural enzymes is stability. In one direct test, freeze-dried antibody-HRP conjugates lost most of their signal within two weeks of dry storage at room temperature, whereas a platinum nanocatalyst kept its signal for weeks, even at 44 C (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). That observation is the main reason given for nanozymes in the studies that follow. The limit addressed in 3.3.2 is the number of coloured product molecules per captured label; the cost is a substrate step, and a label that loses activity in dry storage.

## 3.3.3 Noble-metal nanozymes

Nanozymes are particles with peroxidase-like activity; supplied with hydrogen peroxide and a chromogenic substrate, they oxidise it and deposit colour at the test line. Platinum-based particles dominate the evidence.

A porous platinum core-shell nanocatalyst for the HIV-1 p24 antigen (0.8 pg/mL) is the most thoroughly characterised example (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). Its activity depends on particle size, on the polymer capping used in synthesis and on the porous platinum shell, which stays accessible to hydrogen peroxide even when serum proteins coat the surface; the best size range was 120-200 nm, and larger particles performed worse (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). The same strip works in two regimes: at high antigen the intrinsic dark colour of the particle is visible, and at low antigen the catalytic step takes over, which widens the dynamic range over several orders of magnitude (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). The authors tested serial plasma from an HIV seroconversion panel and detected antigen during the acute phase, and lyophilised reagents survived weeks of accelerated ageing (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.7b06229). The assay needs a wash step and a 5-min development step, so it is not a one-step strip.

Gold-platinum particles follow the same principle. A nanoenzyme-linked sensor with Au@Pt particles for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (0.026 ng/mL; positivity 76.2% against 47.6% for ELISA) reads the developed substrate by transmitted light in a small 3D-printed holder with a laser, a photometer and a smartphone, so the result is colorimetric but not equipment-free (DOI: 10.1016/j.snb.2021.130718). The reported LOD was similar to a laboratory ELISA and lower than that of a conventional strip with the same antibodies, and the Au@Pt particles kept their activity over days while HRP lost it (DOI: 10.1016/j.snb.2021.130718). Platinum nanoparticles have also been used to make amplification reagent-free: in a strip for influenza A, a flow-delay pad and an acidic secondary flow deliver TMB and peroxide to the captured particles after the sample, so the user adds nothing but the sample, and a smartphone application reads the intensity ratio (DOI: 10.1021/acsinfecdis.5c00731). Against RT-PCR on 42 clinical samples tested in triplicate the reported sensitivity was 96.8% and specificity 98.4%, but these figures count the 126 replicate measurements, not 42 independent patients (DOI: 10.1021/acsinfecdis.5c00731). Related noble-metal nanozymes include peroxide-tolerant Au@Pt for potato virus X (4 pg/mL in tuber, 8 pg/mL in leaf) (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2020.121961), oxidase-like Au@Pt nanorods for hepatitis B virus DNA that need no hydrogen peroxide (8.5 pM) (DOI: 10.1039/d1an02084f), trimetallic Au@Pd@Pt for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (0.037 ng/mL, 17-fold lower than the unenhanced strip) (DOI: 10.1039/d2ay01530g), an AuPt nanoalloy for human papillomavirus 16 (HPV16) DNA (DOI: 10.1039/d3ra00757j), Au@Pt for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (40 pg/mL by naked eye, more than 1000-fold against the strip without amplification) and for respiratory syncytial virus F protein (0.78 ng/mL, about 4-fold against colloidal gold) (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-024-06697-3; DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2026.130820), Au@Pt@Pd for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (16-fold and 8-fold against two other strips; sensitivity 84.09%, specificity 100%) (DOI: 10.1007/s00604-024-06819-x), a platinum nanozyme with a mesoporous silica shell for enrichment, which reached 33 copies/mL for influenza A, about 100-fold against gold strips (DOI: 10.1016/j.aca.2024.343474), Pt-Pd particles for foot-and-mouth disease virus serotype O (sensitivity 96.7% on positive serum) (DOI: 10.1016/j.jchromb.2024.124043), PdRu nanocages for HPV16 and HPV18 (0.93 and 0.19 nM) (DOI: 10.1016/j.jcis.2024.06.002), polydopamine-platinum particles for influenza A (2-fold colorimetric and 200-fold catalytic gain against a gold strip) (DOI: 10.1186/s12951-025-03903-z) and the influenza A platinum strip described above, which reached 11.6 pg/mL, 1000-fold lower than the traditional strip. A platinum-polymer nanoparticle strip for SARS-CoV-2 evaluated on 200 clinical specimens had a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 100% (DOI: 10.3390/biomedicines13122993), a reminder that amplification does not remove the limits that antibody pairs and sample type set (Table C1c). The limit addressed in 3.3.3 is turnover per label; the cost is peroxide and substrate, a development step, and in the best-characterised case a wash step.

## 3.3.4 Other nanozymes and structured catalysts

Iron-oxide nanozymes were among the earliest: a Fe3O4 nanozyme strip for Ebola virus glycoprotein, developed with DAB and peroxide after the run, reached 1 ng/mL by eye against 100 ng/mL for a standard colloidal-gold strip and 0.1 ng/mL for an ELISA (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2015.05.025). Because no Ebola patient samples were available, the authors tested pseudo-virus in serum and, as a surrogate, serum from 20 PCR-positive and 31 negative patients with a different bunyavirus infection; the strip detected 12 of 20 positives, against 8 of 20 for the ELISA, with no false positives (DOI: 10.1016/j.bios.2015.05.025). A study on potato viruses shows how differently nanozymes can be used (DOI: 10.3390/plants15040656). Fe3O4 particles were applied as a plain colorimetric label read by eye within 10 minutes, with no substrate step, to build monovalent and bivalent strips for potato viruses X and Y; the authors note that substrate-based amplification could raise sensitivity further, but did not test it (DOI: 10.3390/plants15040656). All 12 tissue-culture plantlets matched RT-PCR, and the authors state that storage stability and batch consistency were not evaluated, even though the abstract mentions storage at ambient temperature (DOI: 10.3390/plants15040656).

Further designs vary the chemistry. A PDA@MnO2 oxidase-like mimic combined with a peptide binder detected the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein at 8.01 pg/mL, 18.7-fold lower than the gold-nanoparticle version of the same peptide strip (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.3c02821). Bimetallic Fe-Co particles were used for porcine rotavirus on faecal samples (100 TCID50/mL, 2-fold better than commercial gold strips; sensitivity 95.45%, specificity 100%) (DOI: 10.1016/j.ab.2026.116259), a nanozyme strip was developed for avian leukosis virus (12.5 TCID50/mL, no cross-reaction with six avian viruses) (DOI: 10.1016/j.tvjl.2025.106508), 3D magnetic multi-metallic nanozymes for influenza A reached 0.8 pg/mL, 125-fold below a commercial colloidal-gold strip, with accuracy 100% (DOI: 10.1002/exp.20240425), and a three-dimensional film-like GO-Pt-AuPt nanozyme detected two respiratory viruses at 1 pg/mL (DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.4c09513) (Table C1d). The limit addressed in 3.3.4 is the cost, stability and reactivity of the catalyst; the cost is again a substrate step.

## 3.3.5 Building the amplification into the strip

The weak point of most amplified strips is the extra step. Three designs try to make it automatic or user-friendly. A nanozyme strip for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein with a push-button signal enhancement in a 3D-printed casing (0.1 ng/mL) worked in undiluted serum (DOI: 10.1039/d1an01350e). A strip with delayed substrate release keeps the chromogenic substrate in a glass-fibre membrane at the sample pad, so it dissolves and reaches the test line only after the nanozyme probes have been captured, which lowers the background and removes a user step (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2025.127557). It was shown on a synthetic influenza A (H1N1) nucleic acid spiked into serum, with a 25-fold lower LOD than the same strip before catalysis, and has not been tested on patient samples (DOI: 10.1016/j.talanta.2025.127557). The influenza A platinum strip described in 3.3.3 uses the same idea with a timed secondary flow (DOI: 10.1021/acsinfecdis.5c00731). An aptamer-based design for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein changes how the chemical signal is transformed to raise signal quality more than 20-fold, with a LOD of 168 pg/mL (DOI: 10.1039/d4nr04130e). These approaches aim to keep the strip one-step, which is the main reason many simple gold strips remain in use (Table C1e).

## 3.3.6 Dual-mode strips and mixed-target assays

Some nanozyme strips add a second readout, such as photothermal, fluorescent or Raman signals, or combine viral and bacterial targets. A Fe3O4@MoS2@Pt nanotag for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein and influenza A (H1N1) gave colorimetric limits of 80 and 20 ng/mL, about 100-fold better than a commercial strip, and photothermal readouts of 10 and 8 ng/mL; the photothermal LODs are not colorimetric and are kept apart in the table (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.3c15851). Others combine nanozymes with fluorescence or SERS: a SQF@ZIF-8/Pt hybrid for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (colorimetric 0.0302 ng/mL) (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c02780), a SERS-nanozyme strip for norovirus (0.001 ng/mL visual) and adenovirus (10^2 PFU/mL visual) (DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03047), and a trimodal strip for norovirus (visual 0.05 ng/mL) and E. coli O157 (DOI: 10.1021/acsami.6c07352). Three strips detect SARS-CoV-2 together with bacteria, or name only two emerging viral targets in the abstract: a magnetic nanozyme for SARS-CoV-2 and two bacteria, a Fe-DAu@Ir strip for SARS-CoV-2 and bacteria that is at least 555-fold more sensitive than a gold-nanoparticle strip, and a corona-structured magnetic nanozyme for two emerging viruses with at least 92.65-fold gain after 2 min of catalysis (DOI: 10.1002/advs.202502574; DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.5c03762; DOI: 10.1021/acs.analchem.6c03394). Only the colorimetric channel and the viral target are counted for this review (Table C1f).

## 3.3.7 What the evidence supports

For the question of this review, catalytic amplification gives the largest changes in the colour signal in the whole review. Within their own papers, the reported gains range from about 2-fold to more than 1000-fold, and the highest values come from nanozyme strips for SARS-CoV-2 (more than 1000-fold for Au@Pt, at least 555-fold for Au@Ir) and influenza A (1000-fold for the platinum strip, 125-fold for the multi-metal nanozyme), while metal deposition and natural enzymes give mostly 5- to 100-fold gains (Table C4). Nanozymes are consistently described as more stable than natural enzymes (Table C2). Three cautions run through the evidence. First, amplification is paid for with steps: substrate, hydrogen peroxide, washing or a reading device; the clearest example is the smartphone nanoenzyme sensor for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, whose result depends on an optical reader. Second, the comparator differs (an unamplified strip, a commercial gold strip, the same strip before catalysis, an ELISA), so fold-improvements cannot be ranked. Third, clinical evidence is thin: where patient samples were tested, sensitivity ranged widely, from 60% for the Ebola-surrogate strip and 74% for the platinum-polymer SARS-CoV-2 strip to high values with small panels, and replicate measurements were sometimes counted as separate samples. The potato virus X and Y Fe3O4 strip shows that a nanozyme can also serve as a simple label without any amplification step, which makes the term "nanozyme strip" ambiguous in the literature.
